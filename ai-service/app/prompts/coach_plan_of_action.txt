
THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Emotional control measures how well a player manages frustration, excitement, anxiety, anger and disappointment during a match. It does not mean showing no emotion. It means recognising the emotion, controlling the response and remaining capable of making sound cricket decisions under pressure.
IF THE EMOTIONAL CONTROL SCORE IS HIGH
•	Reinforce the routine. Identify what helps the player remain composed between balls, after mistakes and during high pressure periods.
•	Test control under pressure. Use competitive scenarios involving close finishes, aggressive opponents, difficult umpiring decisions and changing momentum during practice and training.
•	Develop calm communication. Encourage the player to speak clearly, support teammates and avoid spreading panic or frustration.
•	Protect positive emotion. Allow enjoyment, intensity and celebration without letting excitement become distraction, arrogance or loss of focus.
•	Build leadership. Use the player’s composure to settle the fielding side, support a batting partner or help a bowler recover after a difficult over.
A high score suggests that the player can experience strong emotion without allowing it to control their performance. The coach should now help them use that composure to influence the team positively.
IF THE EMOTIONAL CONTROL SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the trigger. Establish whether the player reacts most strongly to mistakes, dismissal, dropped chances, poor deliveries, criticism, sledging or umpiring decisions.
•	Teach a reset response. Use a simple routine such as step away, breathe, name the emotion and refocus on the next ball.
•	Set behavioural boundaries. Make clear that anger, dissent, blaming teammates and damaging equipment are unacceptable responses.
•	Review without humiliation. Discuss the incident privately, focusing on the behaviour, its impact and a better response next time.
•	Practise pressure deliberately. Recreate difficult situations so the player can rehearse remaining calm and making effective decisions.
A low score should not lead to the player being labelled emotional, difficult or weak. Young cricketers often need help understanding what they feel and how to respond constructively.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Emotional Control Index helps the coach measure whether the player can stay effective when the game becomes personal, frustrating or tense.
High score: reinforce, test and lead.
Low score: identify, reset and regulate.
The goal is not to remove emotion from cricket. It is to ensure that emotion gives the player energy without taking away judgement, discipline or control.


THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Fitness measures whether a player can meet the physical demands of the match and maintain performance as fatigue increases. It includes endurance, speed, strength, mobility, recovery and the ability to repeat high quality efforts. In cricket, match fitness is reflected not only in how hard a player works, but in whether their technique, decision making, movement and concentration remain effective throughout the contest.
IF THE FITNESS SCORE IS HIGH
•	Confirm what supported the performance. Identify the training, recovery, hydration and match day habits that helped the player sustain effort.
•	Increase cricket-specific demands. For example expose the player to longer spells, extended innings, intensive fielding periods and demanding match situations.
•	Monitor skill under fatigue. Assess whether technique, accuracy, decision making and concentration remain reliable late in the game.
•	Develop recovery awareness. Teach the player how to manage energy, hydrate, refuel and recover between overs, spells, innings and matches.
•	Protect against overload. Ensure that strong fitness does not lead to excessive workloads, insufficient rest or avoidable injury.
A high score suggests that the player’s physical preparation supports their competitive performance. The coach should now help the player use that fitness intelligently, sustain standards for longer and recover effectively between demanding efforts.
IF THE FITNESS SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the cause. Establish whether the player struggled because of poor conditioning, illness, heat, inadequate hydration, limited recovery, injury or excessive workload.
•	Separate fitness from effort. Determine whether the player lacked physical capacity or simply failed to apply themselves consistently.
•	Set realistic targets. Create age appropriate goals linked to the player’s role, current ability and stage of physical development.
•	Build progressively. Improve endurance, speed, strength and recovery through gradual, consistent and cricket relevant training.
•	Review match consequences. Show the player how fatigue affected movement, execution, decision making, concentration and involvement.
A low score should not automatically be described as laziness or weakness. Young players develop at different rates, and poor match fitness may reflect workload, growth, recovery or health concerns.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Match Fitness Index helps the coach determine whether the player’s physical condition supports or limits their contribution.
High score: sustain, challenge and protect.
Low score: investigate, individualise and build.
The goal is not merely to finish the match. It is to retain the physical and mental capacity to perform effectively when the game is at its most demanding.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Game awareness measures how well a player understands what is happening around them during a match. It includes awareness of the score, conditions, opposition, field settings, momentum, individual roles and the changing demands of the game. A player with strong game awareness does not simply perform their own skill; they remain connected to the wider contest.
IF THE GAME AWARENESS SCORE IS HIGH
•	Confirm what the player understood. Ask what they noticed about the pitch, field setting, bowler or batter’s plan, scoring rate, match situation and changing momentum.
•	Increase tactical responsibility. Involve the player more in setting fields, planning overs, building partnerships, identifying scoring areas and discussing changes in approach.
•	Develop forward thinking. Encourage the player to anticipate the next delivery, possible field change, bowling adjustment, batting response or shift in the opposition’s tactics.
•	Test awareness under match pressure. Use scenarios involving a rising required rate, wickets falling, a partnership developing, difficult conditions or the final overs of an innings.
•	Keep the player connected to their role. Ensure they use their awareness to support the team’s plan, communicate relevant information and make useful adjustments rather than trying to control every part of the game.
A high score suggests that the player can see beyond their own immediate involvement and understand the direction of the match. The coach should now help the player turn that awareness into clearer communication, stronger anticipation and positive influence on teammates and a potential captain if not already.
IF THE GAME AWARENESS SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify what the player missed. Establish whether they lost track of the score, role, field adjustments, match situation or opposition’s plan.
•	Clarify key information. Teach the player which match details matter most and when they should actively reassess them.
•	Use simple awareness questions. Regularly ask, “What’s happening?”, “What might happen next?” and “What does your role require?”
•	Recreate match situations. Use scenario based games that require players to respond to changing scores, conditions and tactical demands.
•	Review specific moments. Discuss when the player became disconnected from the game and what information could have helped.
A low score does not necessarily mean the player lacks cricket knowledge. They may be concentrating so heavily on their own performance that they stop reading the wider contest. If you develop their thinking tools, it will help them grow their ability to think about the game better.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Game Awareness Index helps the coach assess whether the player understands the match as it unfolds.
High score: confirm, involve and develop anticipation.
Low score: reconnect, question and rehearse.
The goal is not simply to watch the game. It is to recognise what is changing, understand what it means and respond before the opportunity has passed.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
General health measures whether a player is physically and mentally well enough to compete safely and effectively. It includes illness, injury, sleep, nutrition, hydration, recovery, emotional wellbeing and any unusual changes in energy or behaviour. A coach is not responsible for diagnosing health conditions, but must recognise warning signs and place the player’s welfare above the match result.
IF THE GENERAL HEALTH SCORE IS HIGH
•	Reinforce healthy habits. Recognise the sleep, nutrition, hydration and recovery routines that helped the player arrive ready to compete.
•	Monitor throughout the match. Watch for changes in movement, energy, concentration, mood or communication as the game progresses.
•	Encourage honest reporting. Create an environment where the player can report pain, illness or unusual fatigue without fearing loss of selection.
•	Manage workload responsibly. Consider recent matches, school demands, additional coaching, travel, heat and the player’s stage of physical development.
•	Protect recovery. Ensure the player understands what is required after the match to recover physically and mentally.
A high score suggests that the player was healthy, rested and capable of meeting the demands of competition. The coach should continue protecting these habits rather than assuming the player will always be physically and emotionally ready.
IF THE GENERAL HEALTH SCORE IS LOW
•	Stop and assess. Ask appropriate questions about pain, illness, fatigue, sleep, hydration, appetite and emotional wellbeing.
•	Reduce or end participation. Do not allow the importance of the match to override clear signs that the player may be unwell or injured.
•	Communicate with the parent or guardian. Share factual observations calmly without attempting to diagnose the problem.
•	Refer when necessary. Recommend assessment by an appropriately qualified medical or health professional when symptoms persist or appear concerning.
•	Record and review. Monitor whether the issue is isolated or part of a repeated pattern affecting performance, attendance or recovery.
A low score must never be interpreted as weakness or poor commitment. Health problems can affect every other area of performance and must always take priority.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The General Health Index reminds the coach that no match is more important than the welfare of the young cricketer.
High score: reinforce, monitor and protect.
Low score: stop, communicate and refer.
The goal is not to keep the player on the field at all costs. It is to ensure that they are healthy enough to compete, recover and continue developing safely.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Match impact measures how strongly a player influences the course of the game through their performance, decisions, energy and response to important moments. It is not judged only by runs, wickets or catches. A player may affect the match by building a partnership, creating pressure, saving runs, a match changing catch, a few tight overs, supporting a teammate or performing their role when the team needs it most.
IF THE MATCH IMPACT SCORE IS HIGH
•	Identify the decisive contribution. Establish how the player influenced momentum, whether through scoring, taking wickets, fielding, partnerships, pressure or leadership.
•	Connect impact to role. Help the player understand how performing their specific responsibility contributed to the team’s position.
•	Reinforce repeatable behaviours. Focus on the preparation, decisions and actions behind the contribution rather than praising only the final statistics.
•	Increase responsibility. Expose the player to more demanding roles and important match situations where the team requires influence.
•	Monitor consistency. Assess whether the player contributes across different conditions and situations rather than relying on occasional outstanding performances.
A high score suggests that the player did more than participate; they meaningfully changed the match. The coach should help them understand how that impact was created so it can become more deliberate and repeatable.
IF THE MATCH IMPACT SCORE IS LOW
•	Separate impact from statistics. Determine whether the player genuinely contributed poorly or simply had limited opportunity to influence the game.
•	Clarify the player’s role. Ensure they understand how they can affect the match even when runs, wickets or chances are limited.
•	Identify missed moments. Review situations where greater awareness, urgency, communication or courage could have changed the contest.
•	Create purposeful scenarios. Use practice matches that place the player in situations where they must influence momentum or solve a team problem.
•	Set one impact objective. Give the player a clear match responsibility, such as building pressure, protecting a partnership or lifting fielding standards.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Match Impact Index helps the coach assess whether the player’s contribution changed the team’s performance or the direction of the game.
High score: identify, reinforce and expand.
Low score: clarify, involve and create opportunity.
The goal is not to chase personal statistics. It is to recognise the moment, perform the role and make a contribution that matters to the team.

Preparation measures how effectively a player arrives ready to perform in a match. It includes equipment, punctuality, warm-up, hydration, nutrition, mental readiness, role clarity and knowledge of the opposition and conditions. Good preparation does not guarantee success, but poor preparation immediately reduces the player’s chances of performing well.
IF THE PREPARATION SCORE IS HIGH
•	Reinforce the routine. Identify the physical, technical and mental habits that helped the player feel ready before the match began.
•	Increase personal ownership. Encourage the player to manage their own game, hydration, warm up, role preparation and pre match focus.
•	Connect preparation to performance. Help the player recognise how good preparation influenced confidence, concentration and early match execution.
•	Prepare for different conditions. Teach the player to adjust their routine for heat, cold, travel, unfamiliar pitches, delayed starts and changing team roles and questionable umpiring.
•	Monitor consistency. Ensure the player prepares properly for every match, not only finals, trials or games against stronger opposition.
A high score suggests that the player understands that competitive performance begins before the first ball. The coach should help turn these habits into a reliable routine that remains effective regardless of the importance of the match.
IF THE PREPARATION SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the weakness. Establish whether the player was late, poorly equipped, physically underprepared, mentally distracted or unclear about their role.
•	Set clear expectations. Define what the player must complete and understand before arriving at the ground and before play begins.
•	Create a simple checklist. Include equipment, clothing, nutrition, hydration, warm-up, personal role and key match information.
•	Rehearse match-day routines. Practise warm-ups, pre-match discussions and role preparation during practice and training so they become familiar.
•	Review the consequences. Help the player understand how poor preparation affected their confidence, concentration and opening involvement in the match.
A low score should not automatically be viewed as carelessness. Young players may still rely heavily on parents and coaches and need support to develop age appropriate responsibility.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Competitive Preparation Index helps the coach assess whether the player gave themselves the best possible chance to perform.
High score: reinforce, individualise and maintain.
Low score: clarify, organise and rehearse.
The goal is not simply to be present at the ground. It is to arrive ready for the conditions, clear about the role and prepared to compete from the first ball.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Resilience measures how well a player responds to pressure, mistakes, disappointment and setbacks during a match. Cricket constantly tests a player’s ability to recover: a batter may be beaten or dropped, a bowler may concede boundaries, a fielder may miss a chance and a wicketkeeper may make an error. The match situation may turn against the team and players need to dig deep individually and as a group. Resilience is the ability to reset, stay involved and compete effectively in the next moment.
IF THE RESILIENCE SCORE IS HIGH
•	Confirm the response. Ask what the player did after a mistake, difficult period or disappointing outcome to regain control.
•	Reinforce the reset routine. Help the player maintain simple habits such as stepping away, breathing, refocusing and committing to the next ball.
•	Increase the challenge. Expose the player to demanding practice and training scenarios where momentum, pressure and emotions change quickly.
•	Develop leadership under pressure. Encourage the player to remain composed, communicate positively and help teammates recover from setbacks.
•	Monitor emotional control. Ensure resilience does not become emotional suppression, denial or pretending that disappointment does not matter.
A high score suggests that the player can absorb setbacks without allowing one moment to damage the rest of their performance. The coach should now strengthen this response so it remains reliable in more important and difficult matches.
IF THE RESILIENCE SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the trigger. Establish whether the player struggles most after mistakes, poor decisions, umpiring calls, criticism, failure or pressure from the match situation.
•	Separate the moment from the player. Help them understand that one poor ball, shot or fielding error does not define their ability or contribution.
•	Teach a simple reset. Give the player a repeatable process for releasing the previous moment and reconnecting with the next task.
•	Recreate pressure safely. Use competitive scenarios that allow the player to practise responding to mistakes and changing momentum.
•	Review the recovery. Focus not only on what went wrong, but how quickly and effectively the player returned to the contest.

THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Resilience Index helps the coach measure not whether a player makes mistakes, but how they respond afterwards.
High score: reinforce, challenge and lead.
Low score: understand, reset and rebuild.
The goal is not to avoid disappointment. It is to recover quickly enough that the next ball is not controlled by the last one.

Teamwork measures how well a player contributes to the collective performance of the team during a match. It includes communication, support, role awareness, trust, encouragement and a willingness to place the needs of the team alongside personal ambition. In cricket, teamwork is visible in partnerships, fielding energy, backing up, shared plans and how players respond to one another under pressure.
IF THE TEAMWORK SCORE IS HIGH
•	Reinforce team first cricket. Recognise the player who communicates clearly, runs hard for a partner, backs up every throw, supports the bowler after a boundary and remains committed to the team’s plan.
•	Increase cricket responsibility. Involve the player in batting partnership discussions, bowling and fielding plans, between over conversations and helping younger or less confident teammates remain connected to the game.
•	Develop useful match communication. Encourage specific information about the pitch, movement, field changes, scoring options, running opportunities, bowling plans and shifts in momentum rather than empty noise.
•	Protect individual accountability. Ensure the player continues performing their own role whether batting, bowling, fielding or keeping while supporting teammates and contributing to collective plans.
•	Build leadership through visible actions. Use the player’s urgency in the field, body language, backing up, encouragement and composure under pressure to lift the standards and energy of the entire team.
A high score suggests that the player understands that cricket performance is connected. The coach should continue developing this strength so the player becomes someone teammates trust in difficult moments. 
IF THE TEAMWORK SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the cause. Establish whether the player is overly focused on personal performance, unclear about their role, frustrated with teammates or lacking confidence.
•	Clarify team expectations. Explain what effective teamwork looks like in partnerships, communication, fielding support and response to mistakes.
•	Assign specific responsibilities. Give the player clear tasks such as communicating between overs with teammates or supporting a bowler during a difficult spell.
•	Address negative behaviour. Challenge blaming, poor body language, selfish decision making or withdrawal from the contest.
•	Review the impact. Help the player understand how their behaviour affected teammates, momentum and the team’s ability to compete.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Teamwork Index helps the coach assess whether the player improves the performance of those around them.
High score: recognise, involve and develop leadership.
Low score: clarify, connect and build responsibility.
The goal is not simply to play in a team. It is to understand your role, support others and contribute to something greater than your own performance.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Technical execution measures how effectively a player applies their skills during competitive play. It is not simply whether the player succeeds or fails. It considers whether their technique remains controlled, repeatable and appropriate when facing pressure, stronger opposition, changing conditions and the consequences of a real match.
IF THE TECHNICAL EXECUTION SCORE IS HIGH
•	Confirm the process. Identify the movements, routines and decisions that allowed the player to execute effectively under match pressure.
•	Protect what works. Avoid unnecessary technical changes after a strong performance or isolated mistake.
•	Increase the challenge. Expose the player to tougher challenges at practices which reinforce his technical skills and ability.
•	Monitor repeatability. Assess whether good execution is sustained throughout the match, not produced only in occasional moments.
•	Build self-correction. Encourage the player to recognise technical changes and make simple adjustments without waiting for the coach.
A high score suggests that the player can transfer practice skills into competition. The coach must now help make that execution more dependable across different situations, opponents and levels of pressure.
IF THE TECHNICAL EXECUTION SCORE IS LOW
•	Separate outcome from method. Establish whether the player executed poorly or produced a poor result despite having an effective technique.
•	Identify the pressure point. Determine whether the breakdown occurred through technical limitation, fatigue, a mental mistake, brilliant delivery or a bad surface before taking action.
•	Find the recurring fault. Focus on repeated patterns rather than reacting to every individual mistake.
•	Recreate the situation. Design future practices that reproduce the type of delivery, bowler, match situation and pitch conditions that exposed the weakness. This is very important.
•	Give one clear correction. Provide the player with a simple technical focus they can understand and apply in the next performance.
A low score does not automatically mean the player lacks technique. The skill may be present in practice but not yet stable enough to survive match pressure.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Technical Execution Index helps the coach judge whether the player can carry their method from practice into competition.
High score: confirm, protect and stretch.
Low score: separate, diagnose and recreate.
The goal is not technical perfection. It is a technique the player can trust and execute when the match places it under genuine pressure.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Adaptability measures how well a player responds when conditions, instructions, roles or challenges change during practice. Cricket rarely unfolds exactly as planned. An adaptable player can read what is happening, adjust their method and continue performing without becoming confused, frustrated or dependent on constant coaching.
IF THE ADAPTABILITY SCORE IS HIGH
•	Increase the variation. Expose the player to changing conditions, different roles, unfamiliar challenges and unpredictable practice situations.
•	Develop independent thinking. Ask the player what has changed, what adjustment is required and why they have chosen a particular response.
•	Protect the fundamentals. Ensure the player adapts without abandoning the technical and tactical basics that support reliable performance.
•	Test decision-making under pressure. Introduce time limits, changing targets and competitive consequences to challenge the player’s ability to adjust quickly.
•	Encourage leadership. Use the player to help teammates recognise changing situations and respond constructively.
A high score suggests that the player can remain calm, observant and effective when the practice does not go according to plan. The coach should continue stretching this strength while ensuring that adaptability does not become unnecessary experimentation or constant changes of method.
IF THE ADAPTABILITY SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the resistance. Establish whether the player is confused, anxious, technically limited, overly rigid or afraid of making mistakes.
•	Explain what has changed. Help the player recognise the new demand before asking them to adjust their response.
•	Offer limited options. Give the player two or three practical solutions rather than overwhelming them with instructions.
•	Practise controlled variation. Introduce small changes gradually before progressing to more unpredictable and demanding scenarios.
•	Review the response. Ask what the player noticed, what they tried and what they would do differently next time.
A low score may mean that the player performs well only when the practice is familiar and predictable. The coach must teach adjustment as a skill rather than criticising the player for failing to respond immediately.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Adaptability Index helps the coach assess whether a player can think, adjust and remain effective when circumstances change.
High score: stretch, test and empower.
Low score: explain, guide and expose.
The goal is not to change for the sake of change. It is to recognise what the situation demands and make the smallest effective adjustment.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Coachability measures a player’s willingness and ability to listen, reflect, ask questions, accept feedback and apply coaching advice. It does not mean agreeing with everything the coach says. A coachable player takes responsibility for learning, remains open minded and uses feedback to improve rather than defend themselves.
IF THE COACHABILITY SCORE IS HIGH
•	Deepen the conversation. Ask the player what they understood, what they felt and how they intend to apply the feedback.
•	Encourage independence. Give the player opportunities to assess their own performance before offering your opinion.
•	Increase responsibility. Allow the player to set goals, suggest solutions and take greater ownership of their development.
•	Protect individuality. Ensure the player is not simply copying instructions without understanding whether they suit their method.
•	Monitor application. Check whether feedback is being carried into later drills, future sessions and match situations.
A high score suggests that the player is open, reflective and committed to improving. The coach should use this strength to develop a more independent cricketer, not a player who becomes dependent on constant instruction and approval.
IF THE COACHABILITY SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the resistance. Establish whether the player is confused, embarrassed, defensive, fearful of failure or lacking trust in the coach.
•	Improve the delivery. Make feedback clear, specific, age appropriate and focused on behaviour or performance rather than personality.
•	Limit the instruction. Give one useful correction at a time and allow the player enough opportunity to apply it.
•	Invite participation. Ask the player what they noticed, what they think went wrong and what solution they would try.
•	Build trust gradually. Recognise effort, improvement and honest questions so the player feels safe enough to learn from mistakes.
A low score should not immediately be labelled as arrogance or a poor attitude. Sometimes the player does not reject coaching; they reject how, when or why the message is being delivered.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Coachability Index helps the coach assess both the player’s response to feedback and the effectiveness of their own communication.
High score: engage, empower and develop independence.
Low score: understand, connect and rebuild trust.
The goal is not a player who silently accepts every instruction. It is a player who listens carefully, thinks critically and turns useful feedback into meaningful improvement.

Concentration measures a player’s ability to remain mentally present, notice important information and stay connected to the purpose of the practice. It’s not simply about watching the ball. It includes listening, remembering instructions, resetting after mistakes and staying connected during waiting periods, repetition and fatigue.
IF THE CONCENTRATION SCORE IS HIGH

•	Track the pattern. Monitor when concentration drops, what triggers it and how quickly the player can reconnect with the task.

THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Concentration Index helps the coach understand whether a player is mentally engaged or merely physically present.

Low score: investigate, simplify and reset.

Discipline measures a player’s ability to follow instructions, respect standards, control their behaviour and remain committed to the purpose of the practice. It’s not about unquestioning obedience. It is about developing the self-control and responsibility required to practise properly, support teammates and make good decisions even when the coach is not watching.
IF THE DISCIPLINE SCORE IS HIGH
•	Reinforce the behaviour. Recognise the player’s punctuality, effort, self control and respect for the practice environment.
•	Increase responsibility. Give the player leadership tasks, personal targets and greater ownership of their development.
•	Protect independent thinking. Ensure discipline does not become fear, silence or an unwillingness to question and learn.
•	Test consistency. Monitor whether standards remain high during difficult drills, fatigue, disappointment and competitive pressure.
•	Use the example positively. Encourage the player to influence teammates through actions without becoming controlling or judgmental.
A high score suggests that the player understands expectations and can manage their behaviour in the interests of their own development and the team. The coach should continue building responsibility without turning the player into someone who simply follows orders.
IF THE DISCIPLINE SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the cause. Establish whether the behaviour comes from unclear standards, boredom, frustration, immaturity, poor habits or problems outside cricket.
•	Clarify expectations. Explain exactly what acceptable behaviour looks like and why it matters to learning, safety and the team.
•	Set fair consequences. Apply consistent, age appropriate consequences that correct behaviour without humiliating the player.
•	Give the player ownership. Ask the player to reflect on the behaviour, its effect and what they will do differently.
•	Monitor patterns. Record when poor discipline occurs and identify the situations, people or emotions that trigger it.
A low score should not become a permanent label. The coach must address the behaviour clearly while continuing to support the young person behind it.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Discipline Index helps the coach distinguish between a single mistake and a repeated behavioural pattern. It also creates an opportunity to teach standards that extend beyond cricket.
High score: recognise, trust and develop responsibility.
Low score: investigate, clarify and hold accountable.
The goal is not a player who behaves only when watched. It is a player who chooses the right standard because they understand its value.

IF THE GENERAL HEALTH SCORE IS LOW
•	Pause and investigate. Ask simple, appropriate questions about sleep, illness, pain, fatigue, appetite, mood and recent workload.
•	Reduce the demand. Modify or stop the session when the player appears unwell, injured, unusually tired or unable to move safely.
•	Communicate with the parent or guardian. Share factual observations without diagnosing, exaggerating or making assumptions.
•	Refer when necessary. Encourage assessment by an appropriately qualified medical or health professional when symptoms persist or appear concerning.
•	Record and review. Monitor whether the issue is isolated or part of a repeated pattern affecting attendance, performance or wellbeing.
A low score must never be dismissed as weakness, laziness or poor commitment. Health concerns can affect every other performance index and should always take priority over practice outcomes.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The General Health Index reminds the coach that the player comes before the performance.
High score: reinforce, monitor and protect.
Low score: pause, communicate and refer.
The goal is not to keep a young cricketer practising at all costs. It is to ensure that they are healthy enough to practise, recover and continue developing safely.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Intensity measures the energy, purpose and competitive effort a player brings to practice. It is not simply about moving faster, shouting louder or appearing busy. True intensity means practising with the concentration, urgency and commitment required to improve and prepare for competition.
IF THE INTENSITY SCORE IS HIGH
•	Channel the energy. Ensure the player’s effort remains controlled and purposeful rather than rushed, emotional or reckless.
•	Raise the challenge. Introduce competitive targets, time pressure, consequences and match-related scenarios that stretch the player.
•	Protect quality. Monitor whether technical execution and decision-making remain strong as the intensity increases.
•	Encourage leadership. Use the player’s energy to lift standards, support teammates and create a stronger practice environment.
•	Monitor sustainability. Make sure the player can maintain intensity throughout the session without burning out or losing discipline.
A high score shows that the player is prepared to work hard and compete. The coach must now help the player understand that intensity is most valuable when it is focused, controlled and repeatable.
IF THE INTENSITY SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the reason. Establish whether the low intensity is caused by fatigue, poor health, low confidence, boredom, unclear expectations or a lack of motivation.
•	Clarify the standard. Explain exactly what good intensity should look like in movement, effort, communication and response between repetitions.
•	Create short targets. Break the session into smaller, measurable challenges that give the player an immediate purpose.
•	Increase involvement. Use competitive drills, clear roles and regular feedback to keep the player mentally and physically engaged.
•	Review the response. Monitor whether the player’s energy improves when the practice becomes more relevant, demanding and enjoyable.
A low score should not immediately be labelled as laziness or a poor attitude. The coach must first understand what is reducing the player’s engagement before deciding how to respond.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Intensity Index helps the coach distinguish between genuine competitive effort and meaningless activity. It gives the player a clearer understanding of the energy required to improve and allows the coach to design practices that demand purpose rather than simple participation.
High score: channel, challenge and sustain.
Low score: investigate, engage and rebuild.
The goal is not maximum intensity at every moment.
The goal is the right intensity, for the right task, maintained for the right length of time.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
Preparation measures how ready a player is to practise with purpose. 
It includes punctuality, equipment, clothing, hydration, mental and physical readiness and an understanding what the session requires. The player, besides what the coach has in mind, has their own objectives and goals, and what they would like to work on. They don’t need prompting. 
Good preparation begins before the first ball is delivered and gives the player the best possible chance of learning and performing well.
IF THE PREPARATION SCORE IS HIGH
•	Reinforce the routine. Help the player identify the habits and routines that allow them to arrive organised, focused and ready to perform.
•	Increase ownership. Encourage the player to take responsibility for equipment, warm ups, hydration and personal practice goals.
•	Connect preparation to performance. Show the player how good preparation improves concentration, confidence, execution and consistency.
•	Develop match day habits. Use practice routines that can be repeated before matches, trials and important performances.
•	Monitor consistency. Ensure the player prepares properly for every session, not only when highly motivated or expecting individual attention.
A high score shows that the player respects the practice environment and understands that performance begins long before the activity starts. The coach should strengthen these habits until preparation becomes automatic and player led.
IF THE PREPARATION SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify what is missing. Establish whether the problem is poor organisation, unclear expectations, tiredness, lack of support, low motivation or simple forgetfulness.
•	Set clear standards. Explain exactly what the player must bring, wear, complete and understand before each session.
•	Create a simple routine. Give the player a short preparation checklist that can be followed before leaving home and before practice begins.
•	Build accountability gradually. Assign age appropriate responsibility rather than allowing parents or coaches to prepare everything for the player.
•	Review the consequences. Help the player understand how poor preparation reduces practice time, learning quality, safety and team standards.
A low score should not automatically be treated as disrespect or laziness. Young players may need structure, reminders, ideas of good routines and habits and support before they can take full responsibility for their preparation.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
The Preparation Index helps the coach identify whether the player is giving themselves a genuine opportunity to improve.
High score: reinforce, connect and transfer.
Low score: clarify, organise and build responsibility.
The goal is not simply to arrive at practice. It is to arrive ready physically, mentally and practically to make the session count.

THE COACH’S PLAN OF ACTION
IF THE TECHNIQUE SCORE IS HIGH
•	Protect what already works. Do not make technical changes simply to demonstrate that you are coaching.
•	Increase the challenge. Test the player’s technique under greater speed, fatigue, pressure and unpredictability.
•	Develop self-awareness. Ask the player to explain why their method works and what they feel when performing well.
•	Monitor transfer. Check whether the same technical quality appears in competitive practice and matches.
•	Refine rather than rebuild. Make small, purposeful adjustments while protecting the player’s confidence and individuality.
IF THE TECHNIQUE SCORE IS LOW
•	Identify the root cause. Establish whether the problem is technical, physical, mental, tactical or a combination of these.
•	Prioritise one correction. Give the player one clear focus and one simple coaching cue.
•	Simplify the practice. Reduce the speed or complexity until the player can perform the movement correctly.
•	Rebuild through quality repetition. Progress gradually from controlled drills to realistic, pressure-based practice.
•	Review the response. Monitor whether the player understands the correction, gains confidence and improves over several sessions.
THE COACH’S SUMMARY
A high score tells you that the player’s technique is currently a strength. Your responsibility is to protect it, challenge it and help the player understand it more deeply.
A low score tells you that something is limiting the player’s ability to perform the skill consistently. Your responsibility is to diagnose it, simplify it and build an achievable improvement plan.
In both cases, the score must lead to action. It should influence what you coach, how you structure practice, what you monitor and what you discuss with the player.
High score: protect, challenge and refine.
Low score: diagnose, simplify and rebuild.
The Technique Index is not there to tell a young player whether they are good or bad. It is there to show the coach and player what is working, what needs attention and what they should do next.
